title: Writing effective practices
description: Define evidence-bounded criteria, the questions a review answers and the rules that decide, and check them against realistic cases before adoption.
---

A useful practice helps a developer make a specific decision about their work. Its definition must
explain **when the expectation matters, what can be observed, and what would justify feedback**. A
memorable name or a long checklist is not enough.

A practice Hephaestus reviews has two parts. The **criteria** are guidance: the standard, the sources,
what to cite and what other practices own. **How the review decides** holds yes/no questions the review
answers from the evidence, and rules that turn the answers into the outcome. The review never states an
outcome; the first rule that matches its answers does.

Use this guide when creating a custom practice or adapting a catalog entry. The
[Practice Catalog guide](./practice-catalog) explains adoption and updates.

## Start with one expectation

Choose an observable way of working, such as explaining a consequential design decision. Avoid
personality judgments such as “is careful” or whole-system promises such as “writes correct software.”
State who the expectation concerns and what work is being reviewed. An author's reply is not a
reviewer's comment; a plan in an issue is not proof that a change shipped.

When a review runs is separate from whether an expectation applies. A merge event can start a review
without making every practice relevant to that change.

Separate the fields in the editor:

- **What to look for** holds the criteria: the standard, its sources, what to cite and what other
  practices own. It informs every answer but decides nothing.
- **How the review decides** holds the questions and rules: whether the occasion arises, which facts
  meet or fall short of the standard, and how severe a shortfall is.
- **Why it matters** explains the practical benefit without claiming a guaranteed outcome.
- **What good looks like** gives a realistic example. If an example introduces a requirement, also
  state that requirement in the criteria or a question; explanatory guidance does not change review rules.

**How the review decides** appears only while Hephaestus reviews the practice. A practice set to
**Guidance only** has no questions. A practice that needs human review may keep its questions for the
person who answers them.

**Person this practice judges** is the relation to the reviewed work, not the person who wrote the
criteria. A practice about a reviewer's comments must select **reviewer**; leaving **author** selected
would attribute that conduct to the pull-request author. The available relations depend on the kind of
work.

**Only review when** is an optional mechanical gate, separate from the review criteria. An empty gate
means the practice applies to all work of the selected kind. To gate a practice to changed Swift files,
enter:

```json
{
  "skipReason": "the change touches no Swift source file",
  "anyOf": [{ "changedPathMatches": ["**/*.swift"] }]
}
```

`anyOf` is a list of alternatives. Each clause uses exactly one of `changedPathMatches`, `diffContains`,
or `evidenceHasItems`. The explanation in `skipReason` appears when complete evidence proves that none
of the alternatives is present. Clear the field only when the practice must apply to all work. Saving a
name or wording change leaves both the gate and the person judged unchanged.

A gate must be a necessary condition for the practice to apply. A keyword list that misses unfamiliar
APIs or file names cannot establish that no relevant behavior exists. Leave such a gate empty and let a
question decide from the complete captured evidence whether the occasion arises.

## Decide where each decision lives

Use this outline to draft, not as a word-count target.

| Decision      | Where it lives                                                | What the author must make clear                                                                    |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Applicability | A question, usually the first                                 | What fact in the work makes this expectation relevant? What ordinary cases need no observation?    |
| Standard      | **The standard**, and the questions that test it              | What observable requirements must the reviewed work meet?                                          |
| Evidence      | **Sources**                                                   | Which captured sources may establish each claim? Who authored them, and at what point in the work? |
| Absence       | **Grounding**, and the question that rests on it              | What complete, bounded search would justify answering that the needed behavior is missing?         |
| Consequence   | A question that separates the bands, and each rule's severity | What consequence makes a shortfall major or critical rather than minor?                            |
| Outcome       | The rules                                                     | Which combination of answers decides each outcome?                                                 |
| Feedback      | Each rule's reason                                            | What one sentence about the work states what was found?                                            |
| Ownership     | **Defer**                                                     | Which expectations belong to other practices?                                                      |

Prefer “identify a material decision whose rationale the reviewer needs” to a keyword test for
“because.” Prefer the consequence of a missing check to a rule that every missing heading is major.
If a threshold represents a team policy, identify that policy and its exceptions. Do not present a
convenient number as a universal engineering standard.

## Write the criteria

The bundled catalog uses this shape, and it is a good template for custom practices:

1. **REVIEW FOCUS:** — one line naming the observable standard.
2. **## The standard** — what doing that behavior well means, with an example that looks like a lapse
   but meets the standard.
3. **## Sources** — which captured sources can support each claim, and their limits.
4. **## Grounding** — what to cite for each claim, and the boundary an absence search records.
5. **## Defer** — expectations owned by other practices.

Keep each requirement in one place. The criteria do not decide: do not name the outcomes or
severities in them, and do not write an occasion, a verdict or a severity section. Those decisions
belong to the questions and rules, where an author and a developer can see which fact decided. The
bundled catalog has a criteria length limit; meeting it does not establish that the criteria are clear
or correct.

To see the template in use, open **State how to verify the change** in the practice editor. Adapt its
structure, not its applicability rules: each practice needs evidence and consequences that match its
own expectation and the sources your workspace can capture.

### Evidence is more than a matching quotation

A quotation proves that text exists, not that the conclusion follows. Relate instructions to the
actual changed behavior. An authored preview can provide an entry point; implementation text alone
need not communicate the intended result. An uninspected screenshot supplies no established visual
facts. A template issue number is not automatically an adopted requirement.

Required evidence that is missing, failed or truncated stops assessment: record the collection gap,
not an observation about the developer. With qualified evidence, a question the evidence leaves
unresolved stays open. Inability to find a quotation does not itself prove absence. A previous
observation records an earlier judgment; it is not independent proof that judgment was right.

Separate recorded facts from the events they approximate. A commit timestamp records a commit, not
the moment development began. A pull request creation time records a handoff, not an earlier start
time. Do not replace an unknown deciding timestamp with a different event merely to produce an answer.

## Write the questions

A practice asks one to eight questions. The review answers each one **Yes**, **No** or leaves it
**Open**, with one sentence saying why and the lines it rests on, and skips a question the other
answers have already made irrelevant. Each question has a **Title**, the **Question** as the reviewer
reads it, and what **A yes means** and **A no means** for the work.

- **Ask about facts a reviewer can cite.** “Does the description name the failure the change
  removes?” can be settled from cited lines; “Is the description good?” cannot.
- **Write the title as the statement a yes affirms**, such as *Says how to check the main effect*. It
  is shown beside every answer, so it must read correctly next to **Yes**, **No** and **Open**. No
  final period.
- **One concern per question.** A question that bundles two facts gets one answer for both, and the
  rules cannot tell which one failed. Split it, and let a rule combine the answers.
- **Say what counts and what does not.** The question text carries the boundary cases: what a
  restated title, a template placeholder or a reviewer's report of testing count as. Give a concrete
  example in **A yes means** and **A no means**.
- **Ask whether the occasion arises first.** A question such as *The change has an effect a reviewer
  must check* lets the first rule decide **Not applicable**. Answer it **No** only on a fact the review
  can cite.
- **Let a gate question save the review work.** When one answer makes another question unable to
  change the outcome or its severity, whichever way that question would be answered, the review skips
  it. Hephaestus works this out from your rules; there is nothing to configure. Under the **No** above,
  the first rule decides whatever the other questions say, so the review asks none of them. A skipped
  question shows no answer on the observation.
- **Leave the answer open instead of asking whether it is settled.** Do not add a question such as
  *The evidence is unsettled* only to reach **Undetermined**. Say in the question itself when to leave
  the answer open and what to name as settling it: “If the checkout cannot show whether another
  service depends on it, leave the answer open and name what would settle it.” An open answer already
  reaches **Undetermined** wherever it could change the outcome.
- **Name where an absence is searched.** A **No** that rests on something missing records the sources
  searched, what was looked for, and what the search did not cover. The question should say which
  sources make that search complete.
- **Leave the answer open when a deciding source was not captured.** A linked issue, a discussion or a
  checkout that was not collected says nothing about the work. When such a source could supply the
  missing fact, the question says to leave the answer open and name it, never to answer **No**.
- **Say which way a close call goes.** A question whose **Yes** raises the severity ends with *When
  unsure, answer No*, so a borderline case gets the lower band, as the severity table asks. A question
  that can only change the severity, never the outcome, is one the review may leave open; the lower
  band then holds.
- **Keep it short, and write your own examples.** The review applies a short question more reliably
  than a long one: keep long lists in **The standard** and point to them. Invent the examples; a
  question that quotes one real pull request teaches the review that case, not the rule.

A question's key is its stable identifier: rules and recorded answers refer to it. Earlier
observations keep the questions and rules of the revision that judged them.

## Write the rules

Rules are read from the top, and the first rule whose conditions all match the answers decides. Each
rule sets a condition per question — **Any answer**, **Yes** or **No** — and an **Outcome**, a
**Severity** for **Not met**, and a **Reason**. The **Last rule** has no conditions and reads as **In
every other case**, so every combination of answers has an outcome. A practice has two to 32 rules.

Order them from the most specific to the catch-all: the occasion first, then the most severe shortfall,
then lesser ones, then the outcome that holds when nothing else matched.

| Outcome            | Example rule for verification guidance on a persistence change                                                                                               |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Met**            | In every other case: the author's sources give an entry, an action and an expected result.                                                                   |
| **Not met**        | *Says how to check the main effect* is **No**: nothing communicated gives a way to check it.                                                                 |
| **Not applicable** | *The change has an effect a reviewer must check* is **No**: the patch holds only prose or formatting.                                                        |
| **Undetermined**   | A rule may decide it outright when a fact the review can cite makes the practice unanswerable, such as one login that both asks and answers review comments. |

A useful instruction alone does not establish conformance to the complete standard. An optional
improvement being missing is not a shortfall. Do not write two rules that ask for the same correction
under different names.

**Severity** applies only to **Not met**, and follows the consequence, not the number of matches:

| Severity     | Use it when                                                                                   |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Critical** | The consequence is expensive or impossible to undo, such as a leaked credential or lost data. |
| **Major**    | A real problem to fix before the work is considered done.                                     |
| **Minor**    | A bounded shortfall with a specific edit to make; nobody would block the work on it alone.    |

There is no informational severity. A point that does not ask for a change is not a shortfall: let the
work be **Met**, and say what held in the reason.

**The reason is one sentence about the work.** It heads every observation the rule decides and is the
first thing a developer reads about it. State what the work does or lacks — *The title and description
say what changed but not why.* — not what the developer is, what they should do, or which outcome it
is. Ten to 200 characters.

The editor checks the rules as you edit, and the server checks them again on save: the last rule has no
conditions, every rule can decide some combination of answers, and every question is used by a rule.
Saving also refuses a question or reason that names a result label such as `NOT_MET`: say what the
evidence shows in plain words. Those checks prove that the rules are complete, not that they are wise.

### The open answer never becomes a negative

A review leaves a question **Open** when the captured evidence it read does not settle it, and says
what would settle it. Hephaestus then tries the open question both ways. If every way leads to the same
outcome, that outcome stands at the least severe band any of them reached. The observation names the
rule that decided it only when that rule's conditions hold without the open answer; otherwise it says
that the outcome holds however the open questions are settled. If the ways disagree, the outcome is
**Undetermined** and no rule decided it.

So an unsettled fact can never turn into a claim about the work. In the verification example, a
missing check with *Verifying needs more than one bounded fix* left open is still **Not met**, but at
**Minor**, the severity both ways support. Put a question that only raises severity behind the
shortfall it grades, so leaving it open lowers the severity rather than hiding the shortfall.

An open answer is not a capture failure. A source that was never captured records no observation at all.

### The starting questions

A new practice starts with four questions — whether the work gives the practice an occasion, whether
it meets the standard, and whether a shortfall is major or critical — and five rules from **Not
applicable** through **Met**. Existing practices received the same starting questions when Hephaestus
moved to questions and rules. They ask the reviewer to apply the criteria as a whole, so they are only
as precise as the criteria. Replace them with questions about this practice's own facts before relying
on it. **Restore the starting questions** returns to them.

## Challenge the draft before adopting it

Prepare a small set of real or clearly identified synthetic cases. Record the expected answer to each
question, the outcome, the decisive evidence and appropriate feedback **before** running the review.
Include:

- a clearly useful example, a misleading example and a genuinely missing necessary behavior;
- a harmless omission or excluded occasion, and a case with missing required capture;
- sufficient context spread across permitted sources, plus an irrelevant or unadopted linked source;
- an ambiguous case with a precise settling question;
- a near-identical pair where one decisive fact changes, and a cosmetic rewrite that should not
  change any answer;
- source text that attempts to instruct the reviewer, which must remain evidence rather than authority.

Have another reviewer answer the questions without seeing your expected answers. Compare answers
question by question as well as outcomes: matching outcomes can conceal different answers, and a
disagreement on one question shows which question needs rewording. Revise ambiguous questions, then
evaluate on additional cases that were not used to write the revision. Agreement measures consistency;
it does not establish correctness or human independence.

Keep **Review before sending** while evaluating. Check unsupported corrections, missed material
concerns, exclusions and open answers separately; a single positive rate hides these differences.
Record the definition and evidence used for each evaluation. Changing criteria, questions or rules
requires renewed validation, and neither a successful run nor valid citations authorizes automatic
sending.

## Basis for these guidelines

[Google's code-review guidance](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/looking-for.html)
supports checking real functionality, justified complexity, useful tests and explanatory comments.
[Its guidance on small changes](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html)
uses review burden and context rather than a universal line or file limit.
[CheckEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18771) decomposes a judgment into yes/no checklist questions and
reports higher agreement between evaluators.
[Artstein and Poesio's survey](https://aclanthology.org/J08-4004/) explains agreement measures and their
assumptions. [Datasheets for Datasets](https://doi.org/10.1145/3458723) motivates recording composition,
collection and limitations. These sources inform review and evaluation design; they do not prove
that a particular practice or model is accurate.
